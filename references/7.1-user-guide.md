# User guides

A user guide is written for someone who is trying to finish a task, not learn a system. That single fact drives every rule here: organise by task, lead with the goal, keep sentences short, and never explain the architecture.

The reader is usually mildly frustrated when they arrive. Write for that person.

## Document template

```markdown
# <Product or area> — User guide

<metadata table, plus:>
| **Audience** | End users / Admins / Support |
| **Applies to** | Version … |

<plain-language summary paragraph: what the product does, in one or two sentences>

## Before you start
What the reader needs: an account, a role, data already in the system.

## <Task 1>
## <Task 2>
One H2 per task, phrased as a goal the user has.

## Common questions
| Question | Answer |
|---|---|

## Troubleshooting
| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|

## Glossary
Only terms specific to the product, only if there are more than five.

## Changelog
```

Task sections are ordered by how often they are done, not by how the software is structured. The menu order in the application is a poor guide to what people actually need.

Each task section follows the same internal shape:

```markdown
## Export a report

Use this to send monthly figures to someone who does not have an account.

1. Open **Reports** from the left menu.
2. Choose a date range, then select **Export**.
3. Pick **CSV** or **PDF** and select **Download**.

The file downloads to your browser's usual location. Exports include only the columns visible in your current view — adjust columns before exporting if something is missing.
```

Goal sentence, numbered steps, then a short note covering the results and the one thing that surprises people. That closing note is where most support tickets are prevented.

## Writing steps

- One action per step. A step containing "and then" is two steps.
- Start with the verb, in the imperative: "Select **Save**", not "You should then click on the Save button".
- Name UI elements exactly as they appear, in bold: **Save**, **Settings → Billing**. If the label in the product is unclear, fix the product rather than explaining it in the guide.
- Say where things are when it is not obvious: "in the top right", "under **Advanced**".
- Keep a task under about seven steps. Longer means it is really two tasks, or the product needs simplifying — note the latter to the product owner rather than papering over it.
- Write what the reader sees after the last step, so they know it worked.
- Cover the recoverable mistake at the end of the task ("If you exported the wrong range, repeat with a new range — nothing is overwritten"), not in a separate troubleshooting entry.

Warnings are used rarely and only for irreversible actions:

> Deleting a workspace removes all of its projects. This cannot be undone.

One warning on a page is read; four are ignored.

## Screenshots and visuals

Screenshots go stale faster than any other content, so include one only where words genuinely fail — a screen with an unfamiliar layout, or a small control that is hard to describe.

When a screenshot exists, crop it to the relevant region rather than showing the full window, and describe the target in text as well so the step still works when the screenshot drifts. Keep images in an `images/` folder next to the document and reference them relatively.

Where a process spans several screens, one small Mermaid flowchart at the top of the task often replaces three screenshots and stays correct much longer.

## Choosing what to document

Not everything a product can do belongs in the guide. Prioritise:

1. The tasks most users do most often.
2. Tasks that are rare but high-stakes — billing changes, permissions, deletion, data export.
3. Anything support is repeatedly asked about; that list is the best available evidence of where the product is unclear.

Skip anything self-evident from the interface. A guide that documents every button trains readers to stop reading it.

## Guides for AI features

Users approach AI features with either too much or too little trust, and the guide is where that gets calibrated. Cover:

- What the feature is good at, in concrete examples rather than adjectives.
- What it is not reliable for, stated plainly.
- What the user should check before acting on output, especially for anything with financial, legal or safety consequences.
- How to get better results — the two or three input habits that actually change the output.
- What happens to their input: whether it is stored, and whether it is used for training.
- What to do when output looks wrong: how to report it, and whether there is a manual path to the same outcome.

Written honestly, this section prevents both the user who trusts everything and the user who abandons the feature after one bad answer.

## Tone

Plain English, short sentences, present tense, second person ("you"). Avoid "simply", "just" and "easily" — when a step is not easy for the reader, these words read as blame. Avoid internal vocabulary entirely: users have accounts and files, not tenants and blobs. If an internal term leaks into the UI, document it once in the glossary and use it consistently thereafter rather than inventing a friendlier synonym that appears nowhere on screen.
