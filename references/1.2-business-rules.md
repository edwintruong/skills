# 1.2 Business rules and glossary

The domain's decision logic and its vocabulary. Rules are the part of the domain engineers get wrong most often, because they are usually communicated verbally and in fragments; the glossary is what stops two departments meaning different things by the same word.

## Business rules

Rules are the part of the domain that engineers get wrong most often, because they are usually communicated verbally and in fragments. Give them their own document per area and write each one as a table row that a tester can turn directly into a case.

```markdown
## Loan eligibility

| # | Rule | Condition | Outcome | Source | Effective from |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BR-EL-01 | Minimum age | Applicant age < 20 | Reject, reason `under_age` | Credit policy §2.1 | 2026-01-01 |
| BR-EL-02 | Income floor | Monthly income < 8,000,000 VND | Reject, reason `low_income` | Credit policy §2.3 | 2026-01-01 |
| BR-EL-03 | Existing debt | Debt-to-income ≥ 60% | Manual review | Credit policy §3 | 2026-04-01 |
```

Points that repeatedly cause defects and are worth stating explicitly:

- **Boundaries.** Write `<` or `≤` rather than "under" — off-by-one at a threshold is the single most common business-rule bug.
- **Order of evaluation.** When several rules can fire, say which wins and whether evaluation stops at the first match.
- **Units and currency**, always, including for percentages (of what?).
- **Effective dates.** Rules change; a rule with no effective date makes historical data impossible to explain.
- **Rounding.** For anything monetary, state the rounding rule and the point in the calculation where it applies.

Where the logic is genuinely a decision tree, add a small decision table instead of prose — combinations of conditions across columns make missing cases visible in a way sentences never do. A decision table is mandatory when there are at least two conditions. It carries two things prose cannot: **all 2ⁿ combinations** (including impossible combinations, with reasons) and the **hit policy** when several rows match — `U` · `F` · `P` · `C`, answering the question *"which rule wins?"* in a testable form. Process and example: [0.5 K6](0.5-derivation.md#k6-decision-tables-and-hit-policy); every table row produces at least one test case in [6.1](6.1-test-plan.md).

For AI-assisted decisions, state which part of the decision the model makes and which part remains rule-based, plus what happens when the model is unavailable. A business rule document that silently depends on a model is a compliance problem waiting to happen.

## Domain glossary

One file per product. Alphabetical, and deliberately opinionated:

| Term | Definition | Not to be confused with |
|---|---|---|

The third column is what makes a glossary useful rather than decorative. Domains almost always contain near-synonyms that different departments use differently — *customer* vs *account holder*, *order* vs *booking*, *cancelled* vs *voided* — and writing down the distinction once prevents a class of bugs that is otherwise very hard to trace.

Terms in the glossary are the terms used in schemas, API fields and UI copy. When the glossary and the code disagree, one of them is a defect; say which in the changelog.
